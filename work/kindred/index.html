Kindred

A privacy-first AI health companion for people living with chronic conditions.

  • Client: OpenMined
  • 8 months
  • Team of 4
  • Role: UX Designer

Turned an open-ended brief into a clinician-validated product concept, ready for development, in 8 months.

Problem
OpenMined had built technology that lets AI compute with personal data on a person's own device, safely, but it had no consumer product and no use case that made it matter to everyday people. Meanwhile, 58.5% of US adults already search online for health information, trading their privacy for answers they can't verify.
Role
UX Designer
Process
Two rounds of the double diamond with my team of four: from technology to 36 ideas to one use case, then from 36 features to 17 worth testing to the 4 feature sets that mattered.
Outcome
Kindred, a privacy-first AI health companion for people living with chronic conditions. It brings scattered health records into one private timeline, where AI and verified patients with the same diagnosis help make sense of them. Patients rated their likelihood to use it 8/10, and clinicians 8.5/10.

Diamond 1Discover and Define

Finding the right problem

01Discover

We started with almost nothing.

OpenMined gave us three things: their mission, a goal of reaching consumers, and their website. The mission read like this:

A non-profit community building technology that enables secure computation across siloed data, unlocking collective intelligence while preserving attribution-based control (opens in a new tab).

OpenMined

02Discover

Before we could narrow down, we had to understand the technology.

OpenMined's privacy technology lets AI compute with personal data on your own device, safely. The data never leaves your hands; only the insight it produces is shared.

I spent a month on desk research into secure computation and decentralized communities, reading 50 primary research articles and 73 secondary papers.

Alongside it, I ran a survey on how people feel about AI and privacy. It drew 82 responses.

03Discover

Once we understood it, we saw possibilities everywhere.

Through SME interviews and generative workshops (Crazy 8s, How Might We, and more), we generated 36 use case ideas: from a mental health tool built on private health records, to an elderly health kit, to a shopping recommendation platform.

04Define

Then came the hard part: choosing one.

I plotted the ideas on an impact-versus-risk matrix.

Then I ran 50 speed-dating intercepts to test how people felt about the top 8.

05Define

People already ask the internet about their health. They deserve answers that are private and accurate.

That idea became The Informed Patient. It scored highest on our weighted decision matrix, which tested each of the top 8 against criteria such as urgency of need, technical fit, and legal constraints.

Most people already go online with health questions, so this was the use case where health privacy and AI accuracy mattered most.

Diamond 2Develop and Deliver

Building the right thing

06DevelopLow fidelity

We designed for sharing. Patients wanted understanding.

We assumed OpenMined's technology was best suited to a network of patients with the same condition sharing experiences, with the system showing them how many people their data had helped.

I tested that over three rounds, from low fidelity to a working prototype, with over 30 participants. In low fidelity, we sketched 36 possible features and brought them to co-design sessions with 11 people, who narrowed them to the 17 worth testing.

Those sessions, and interviews with clinicians, showed otherwise. Patients didn't want to feel watched. They wanted insight from others' data and their own, with the help of AI, personalized to their condition.

InsightFeatures built around sharing data gave way to features that help people understand their own health.

07DeliverMid fidelity

We designed with patients and clinicians, not just for them.

Through co-design sessions and design workshops, patients and clinicians shaped the product alongside us.

We built the 17 features into mid-fidelity screens and tested them again. I also made sure clinicians were comfortable with the direction.

InsightWe combined the 17 features into 4 feature sets. Each one helps patients understand their health, and each keeps the promise of OpenMined's technology: insight is shared, but the data never moves.

  • Community Q&A

    Answers from patients with a verified diagnosis, ranked by how closely they match you.

  • Timeline of You

    Every diagnosis, lab and prescription from connected providers, on one private timeline.

  • Learning Modules

    Short explainers that turn numbers like eGFR into what they mean day to day.

  • AI Search

    Plain-language answers with every source shown. Context, never a diagnosis.

08DeliverHigh fidelity

Patients and clinicians both said they were likely to use the working prototype.

The final round was a working prototype, tested with patients and clinicians. Try it here, or open it in a new tab (opens in a new tab).

ImpactPatients rated their likelihood to use the working prototype 8/10. Clinicians rated it 8.5/10.

09Deliver

We gave it a name.

My team and the clients voted on a name for the product. The winner: Kindred.

10Deliver

From an open-ended brief to a product ready to build, in eight months.

Kindred unifies scattered health records into one private timeline, where AI and patients with similar diagnoses help make sense of them. A medically trained AI is part of the concept, not yet a validated capability. It runs on OpenMined's attribution-based control (opens in a new tab), so insights are shared without the underlying data ever moving.

We handed over the product, a go-to-market strategy, a build sequence and our discovery framework, which OpenMined can rerun on future ideas. OpenMined's CEO, Andrew Trask, responded enthusiastically, and Madhava, the principal engineer leading BioVault, validated our technical direction.

Reflection

The biggest shift was realizing this was never a data-permission problem. It was a trust problem: people didn't need to understand the privacy technology, they needed to feel safe at the moment they shared something.

Our first idea came from what the technology could do, not from what patients needed. Co-design corrected that, and next time I would bring patients in before the first diamond closes, not after.

I would also test with clinicians from the very first round, since their comfort decided whether Kindred could work in real care.
